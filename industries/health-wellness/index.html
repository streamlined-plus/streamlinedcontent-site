Winners and losers

Same specialty, same zip code. Two very different calendars.

The gap isn't clinical quality. It's whether a search engine, a map, or an AI can find, trust, and quote you — and whether the patient can book in one tap when it does.

The practice that gets chosen

  • Says the same thing everywhere. Hours, providers, insurance and services match on Google, Apple Maps, Healthgrades and the site — so engines trust and cite it.
  • Answers the question on the page. "Do you take my insurance?" "How soon can I be seen?" — answered in the first sixty words, structured so AI can lift it.
  • Asks for reviews, then answers them. A steady flow of recent reviews and a human reply on each one.
  • Books in one tap. Online scheduling, instant confirmation, reminders — no phone tag.
45% value providers who respond to reviews · 74% will leave one when prompted — rater8 2025

The practice that gets scrolled past

  • Listings disagree. Old hours on one profile, a wrong phone on another — the engine sees a risk, not a source.
  • One "services" page for everything. Nothing specific enough to quote, so the AI cites someone else.
  • Reviews go stale and unanswered. Under four stars, or only 12 reviews from 2023 — a 53% wall.
  • "Call during business hours." The patient wanted to book at 9 pm. They booked the competitor.
40% have canceled or changed plans based on reviews alone · 82% give a practice 1–2 chances before switching

Humans in the loop · clinical review

AI drafts the page. A clinician signs off on every claim.

Medical marketing has a higher bar. Nothing about a condition, a treatment, an outcome or a price ships without a named reviewer — and nothing patient-identifying ever touches a model.

  • No PHI, ever. Content systems run on your public expertise, never patient data.
  • Claims stay defensible. Outcomes and comparisons are reviewed against what you can support.
  • Reviews get a human voice. Responses that are warm, specific, and never disclose a visit.
